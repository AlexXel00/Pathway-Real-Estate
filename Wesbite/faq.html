Buying basics

Can foreigners buy property in the Philippines?

Yes, and it is simpler than many people expect. A condominium unit you can buy directly in your own name, as long as the building's foreign quota is not yet full.

For land, the usual route is a Philippine corporation in which you hold up to 40% of the shares. It is a well-established and secure structure, and the standard way foreign-owned hotels and resorts here hold their land. We set it up for you together with our partner lawyers.

Alternatively, you can secure land with a long-term lease (we offer 50 years, in two 25-year terms). Our Foreign Buyers guide explains both routes in detail.

How long does a purchase take?

For a clean, titled property, the transfer usually takes several weeks to a few months after both sides sign the Deed of Absolute Sale. Most of that time goes into tax processing at the BIR (Bureau of Internal Revenue) and the issuance of the new title at the Registry of Deeds.

Untitled land, missing documents or an estate that still has to be settled can extend this considerably. We check this before you commit, so you know what to expect.

Do I need to be in the Philippines to buy?

Not necessarily. You can sign through a Special Power of Attorney (SPA) in favour of a person you trust. If you sign the SPA abroad, it has to be notarised and apostilled in the country where you sign it. We recommend visiting the property at least once before you buy.

Can I get a bank loan as a foreigner?

In practice, Philippine bank financing for foreign buyers is limited. Most of our foreign clients pay in cash or finance the purchase from their home country. Some condo developers offer payment plans spread over the construction period, which can make pre-selling units easier to finance.

Titles & land

What do the different title types mean?

The type of document behind a property is the single most important thing to check in Palawan. These are the ones you will come across most often:

OCT (Original Certificate of Title)
The first title ever issued for a piece of land, after it was registered for the first time. Strong proof of ownership.
TCT (Transfer Certificate of Title)
The title issued whenever titled land changes hands or is subdivided. The most common title for private land. Strong proof of ownership.
CCT (Condominium Certificate of Title)
The title for an individual condo unit. This is what a foreign condo buyer receives.
A&D (Alienable and Disposable)
Land that the government has classified as available for private ownership, but for which no title has been issued yet. It can be titled, but that takes time and has to be checked carefully.
Tax Declaration
A record that someone declares and pays real property tax on the land. It is not a title and does not prove ownership on its own.
CLOA / EP
Titles issued under agrarian reform. They come with restrictions on selling and transferring, so they need a lawyer's review before any purchase.
Rights / "rights claim"
An informal claim to occupy or use land, without title. The weakest position of all and a common source of disputes.
What does "Titled" or "A&D" mean on your listings?

Titled means the property has an OCT or TCT, so ownership is registered at the Registry of Deeds. A&D means the land is classified as alienable and disposable but not yet titled. A&D land can be a good opportunity, often at a lower price, but the titling process takes time and needs proper due diligence. We tell you openly which situation applies to every listing.

How do you check that a title is genuine and clean?

Before any purchase, we check at least the following:

  • A certified true copy of the title from the Registry of Deeds, compared with the owner's copy
  • Annotations on the title, such as mortgages, liens or adverse claims
  • The land classification with the DENR, to make sure the land is not forest land or part of a protected area
  • The ECAN zone of the property with the PCSD (see Building in Palawan below)
  • Paid-up real property taxes

If you would like the boundaries confirmed on the ground, we can arrange a relocation survey by a licensed geodetic engineer. The cost of the survey is covered by the buyer.

Costs & taxes

What costs do I pay on top of the purchase price?

By common practice in the Philippines, the buyer pays:

  • Documentary Stamp Tax: 1.5%
  • Transfer tax: up to 0.5% in provincial municipalities such as El Nido or San Vicente (including Port Barton), and up to 0.75% in cities such as Puerto Princesa
  • Registration fee at the Registry of Deeds: roughly 0.25%, based on a graduated table
  • Notarial and legal fees, which vary

The seller usually pays the 6% Capital Gains Tax. All taxes are calculated on the highest of the selling price, the BIR zonal value or the assessor's fair market value. Who pays what can be negotiated, so it should always be written into the contract.

Can you give me an example?

For a lot with a value of PHP 5,000,000 in a provincial municipality, a buyer should budget roughly:

  • Documentary Stamp Tax (1.5%): PHP 75,000
  • Transfer tax (0.5%): PHP 25,000
  • Registration fee (about 0.25%): around PHP 12,500

That is about PHP 112,500, plus notarial and legal fees. These are estimates. We prepare an exact cost overview for every property before you sign.

Is there an annual property tax?

Yes, the real property tax. It is charged on the assessed value, which is a percentage of the market value set by the local assessor. The rate is up to 1% in provinces and up to 2% in cities, plus 1% for the Special Education Fund. Because it is based on the assessed value rather than the full market value, the yearly amount is usually modest.

Building in Palawan

What permits do I need to build?

Palawan has one extra layer that the rest of the country does not have. The usual order is:

  • SEP Clearance from the Palawan Council for Sustainable Development (PCSD). It confirms that your project is allowed in the ECAN zone of the property, and it is required before other permits are issued.
  • Environmental clearance from the DENR (an ECC or a Certificate of Non-Coverage), depending on the size and type of the project
  • Zoning or locational clearance from the municipality or city
  • Building permit from the local Office of the Building Official, together with the electrical and sanitary permits
  • Certificate of Occupancy once construction is finished

If the property lies inside a declared protected area, an additional clearance from the Protected Area Management Board is needed. We handle this process for our clients as part of our services.

What is ECAN and why does it matter?

ECAN (Environmentally Critical Areas Network) is Palawan's zoning system under the Strategic Environmental Plan for Palawan (RA 7611). It divides land and sea into zones:

  • Core zones are strictly protected. No development is allowed.
  • Buffer zones (restricted and controlled use) allow only limited activities.
  • Multiple-use zones are where most residential and tourism development happens.

A property can have a valid title and still sit in a zone where you cannot build what you have in mind. That is why we check the ECAN zone before you buy, not after.

What are the rules close to the beach?

Under the Water Code of the Philippines, a strip along the shore is a public easement: 3 metres in urban areas, 20 metres in agricultural areas and 40 metres in forest areas. The foreshore itself is public land. You cannot fence off or build on these areas, even if they appear on a sketch. For beachfront land, we always confirm where the usable area actually begins.

Corporations & structures

Can I set up a corporation to own land?

Yes. A Philippine corporation can own land as long as at least 60% of its shares are held by Filipinos, while you as a foreigner can hold up to 40%.

It sounds more complicated than it is. Together with our partner lawyers, we take care of the whole process for you, from structuring the company to registering it and buying the land.

For many private buyers, a 50-year lease is an even simpler option. Our Foreign Buyers guide compares both.

How much does it cost to set up a corporation?

The government fees themselves are modest. For a small corporation, the SEC fees (name reservation, filing fee, by-laws), BIR registration and local permits typically come to around PHP 10,000 to 15,000.

The bigger part is professional fees for the lawyer and accountant who prepare and file everything. All in, a small corporation often ends up around PHP 85,000 to 120,000. After that, plan for yearly costs: bookkeeping, audited financial statements, annual filings with the SEC and the renewal of the business permit.
